This week, I started working on the third AI-powered study tool for StudySync: **Multiple Choice Questions**!

After completing Flashcards last week, I wanted to build a study tool that goes beyond recalling information and allows students to practice applying their understanding of course concepts.

Most of my time this week was spent developing, refining, and testing the Gemini prompt responsible for generating multiple-choice questions from student-provided NLP notes. So far, I've worked through **four versions of the prompt**, making adjustments based on the quality of the generated questions.

# **What I Worked On**

I started by outlining what I wanted the Multiple Choice tool to accomplish.

Like Flashcards, Multiple Choice will use the student's submitted course notes to generate up to ten questions. However, each question will include four answer choices, one correct answer, and an explanation.

I wanted the questions to test more than simple memorization, so I focused on several requirements:

- Generate questions covering different concepts from the original notes.
- Include a variety of question types, such as factual recall, conceptual understanding, comparison, and interpretation.
- Create plausible incorrect answers, also called distractors.
- Make sure each question has exactly one defensible correct answer.
- Provide explanations that help students understand why an answer is correct.
- Avoid introducing information that is not supported by the submitted material.

I also wanted Gemini to generate fewer than ten questions when the notes don't contain enough information to support ten distinct, meaningful questions.

# **Prompt Engineering and Testing**

The biggest part of my work this week involved experimenting with different versions of the Multiple Choice prompt.

I started with instructions for generating questions and answer choices, then gradually added more specific requirements to improve the quality of the output.

One challenge was balancing question variety with accuracy. I wanted Gemini to generate questions that require students to think about the material rather than simply recognize a definition. However, more complicated questions also create opportunities for ambiguity or unsupported information.

Another challenge involved the distractors. If the incorrect answers are obviously wrong, students may be able to identify the correct answer without actually understanding the concept.

I revised the prompt to encourage distractors that are plausible and related to the material, while still ensuring that only one answer is correct.

I also added instructions for Gemini to identify the important concepts and learning objectives in the notes before generating questions. My goal was to reduce repetitive questions and improve coverage across the submitted material.

By the fourth version, the prompt included more detailed requirements for source accuracy, question selection, variety, and answer quality.

I began testing the generated questions to see how well these instructions translated into actual results.

# **Evaluating AI-Generated Questions**

Something I've been thinking about throughout this project is how to determine whether AI-generated study materials are actually useful.

With Flashcards, I focused primarily on accuracy, topic coverage, and repetition. Multiple Choice introduces some additional considerations.

A question can be factually correct but still be a poor assessment question. For example, the answer might be obvious because the distractors are unrelated to the topic, or the wording might unintentionally reveal the correct choice.

I've been paying closer attention to whether questions:

- Are supported by the original notes.
- Test a meaningful learning objective.
- Have exactly one correct answer.
- Include plausible distractors.
- Provide accurate and useful explanations.
- Cover different concepts without unnecessary repetition.

This has made evaluating Multiple Choice more complicated than I initially expected.

It has also reinforced something I noticed while developing the Review Guide and Flashcards: **getting an AI model to produce the requested output is not the same as getting it to produce high-quality output.**

# **Challenges and Reflection**

One of my biggest challenges this week has been figuring out how specific to make the prompt.

Adding more instructions can help address problems in the generated questions, but it can also make the prompt increasingly complicated. I've been trying to make changes based on specific issues I notice during testing rather than adding requirements without a clear purpose.

I've also been thinking more about the difference between generating study materials and evaluating educational content. Multiple-choice questions need to be accurate, but they also need to be appropriately challenging and useful for learning.

This process has given me more experience with **prompt engineering, structured output design, and AI evaluation**, which are areas I'm particularly interested in continuing to develop.

Although I haven't finished implementing the Multiple Choice interface yet, I feel like the time spent refining the prompt will help establish a stronger foundation for the feature.
