# What I Worked On

I started by identifying several things I would like from my next career. I want a role where I can spend a significant amount of time independently solving technical problems and building things, while still working as part of a team and learning from more experienced engineers. I prefer remote work, although hybrid work in Colorado is also an option. I am especially interested in work that has a meaningful purpose and would like a career with room to grow technically and professionally.

For each career, I researched the typical work, technologies and skills, entry-level expectations, current job market, compensation, and possible portfolio projects. I also considered how each career fits my current skills and what I would need to learn.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>Software / Full-Stack Engineer</strong></summary>

Software engineers design, build, test, and maintain software applications. Full-stack engineers work across both the user-facing and server-side portions of an application, which can include interfaces, APIs, databases, application logic, and testing.

Common technologies include Python, Java, JavaScript/TypeScript, SQL, React, cloud platforms, Git, and Docker. The exact technology stack varies considerably between employers. According to O*NET employer-posting data, Python, Java, SQL, JavaScript, cloud platforms, Git, React, Docker, HTML, and CSS are among the technologies currently requested for software developers.

This career aligns well with my existing experience in Python, Flask, HTML/CSS, databases, Git/GitHub, testing, and Agile development. I also have some experience with JavaScript and PostgreSQL. Areas I could strengthen include JavaScript/TypeScript, React, APIs, cloud deployment, Docker, and production application development.

I am particularly interested in full-stack development because I like both the logical problem-solving involved in backend development and the design aspects of building user-facing applications. I also like the breadth of software engineering because it could allow me to work in many different industries. One concern is the increasingly competitive entry-level software market and how AI-assisted development is changing expectations for junior developers.

<summary><strong>Applied AI/ML Engineer</strong></summary>

Applied AI/ML engineers use existing AI and machine-learning capabilities to build applications and product features. Rather than primarily developing new models, they may integrate language models and other ML systems into applications using APIs, retrieval systems, agents, embeddings, and other tools.

This work combines many traditional software-engineering skills with newer AI technologies. Python, APIs, databases, cloud platforms, Docker, and software-development practices remain important, while additional technologies can include model APIs, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), vector databases, embeddings, agents, and AI evaluation tools.

This is one of the careers I have found most interesting. I like that it combines application development with AI/ML rather than requiring the model itself to be the center of my work. Some of the technologies, including RAG, model APIs, and Docker, are new to me, but I have encountered them while looking at AI applications and would like to understand them better. I am also comfortable with Applied AI being a direction I move toward after first gaining professional experience as a software developer.

A portfolio project for this path could involve building an application around a specific problem and incorporating an AI capability rather than simply creating a generic chatbot.

<summary><strong>ML / NLP Engineer</strong></summary>

Machine Learning Engineers work more directly with models, training systems, datasets, and ML infrastructure. NLP Engineers specialize in systems involving language, such as text classification, embeddings, information retrieval, language models, and other forms of language processing.

Typical responsibilities can include preparing datasets, training models, tuning hyperparameters, comparing algorithms, evaluating performance, building data pipelines, and deploying and monitoring models. Python is especially important, along with tools such as scikit-learn, PyTorch, TensorFlow, pandas, NumPy, and cloud/ML infrastructure.

My current Machine Learning and Natural Language Processing courses are giving me experience with many of the foundations behind this career, including regression, classification, model evaluation, generalization, tokenization, language models, and NLP.

I have enjoyed working with models and experimenting with parameters to improve their performance. However, I am less interested in a career where statistics and data preparation make up a large portion of my work. I would rather use ML as part of building a product than have model and data development be my primary responsibility. I would not completely rule this path out, but Applied AI currently sounds more aligned with my interests.

<summary><strong>AI Evaluation & Responsible AI</strong></summary>

AI Evaluation and Responsible AI cover several related specialties rather than one standardized job title. Technical evaluation engineers develop methods for determining whether AI systems are accurate, reliable, safe, and behaving as intended. Responsible AI roles can extend into questions involving bias, fairness, transparency, privacy, governance, risk, and appropriate use.

The technical side can involve developing benchmarks, testing hallucinations and failure cases, evaluating model responses, red teaming, and creating automated evaluation systems. Other Responsible AI roles lean more heavily toward governance, documentation, policy, risk assessment, and collaboration.

This area is particularly interesting to me because I am interested in the broader questions surrounding how AI should be developed and used. I like the idea of working with AI while also considering its biases, limitations, usefulness, and effects on people. After researching the field further, I think I am more interested in the Responsible AI and ethical/governance questions than in spending most of my time developing automated model-testing systems.

Dedicated entry-level positions appear relatively limited, so I would be comfortable treating this as a potential specialization after gaining experience in software development or Applied AI rather than expecting it to be my first job.

<summary><strong>Accessibility Engineer</strong></summary>

Accessibility Engineers and specialists work to make software usable by people with disabilities. Depending on the position, responsibilities can include accessible front-end development, WCAG compliance, screen-reader and keyboard testing, accessibility audits, automated accessibility testing, and helping other developers remediate accessibility problems.

Common technologies include HTML, CSS, JavaScript, React, ARIA, automated accessibility tools, and assistive technologies such as screen readers. Accessibility can also overlap with QA, UX, front-end engineering, and consulting.

Although I think accessibility is an important part of good software development, this was one of the careers that interested me least. I do not want to specialize primarily in front-end development or spend a large portion of my work auditing interfaces against accessibility standards. I would rather consider accessibility as an important responsibility when building software than make it my primary specialization.

<summary><strong>Solutions Engineer / Developer Relation</strong></summary>

Solutions Engineers combine technical expertise with customer-facing work. They may meet with customers, understand technical requirements, demonstrate products, build proofs of concept, assist with integrations, and work with sales and engineering teams. Developer Relations roles similarly combine technical knowledge with communication but focus more on helping developer communities through tutorials, documentation, sample applications, presentations, and community engagement.

My previous teaching and communication experience would transfer well to this type of work. However, researching the actual responsibilities made it clear that this career does not align with what I want from my career change.

One reason I prefer remote technical work is that I want to move away from customer-facing responsibilities. I would rather spend most of my time independently working on technical problems, with a team to collaborate with and experienced engineers I can learn from. I am not interested in having customers, sales conversations, or a developer community as a major part of my responsibilities.

<summary><strong>QA Automation Engineer / SDET</strong></summary>

QA Automation Engineers and Software Development Engineers in Test (SDETs) use programming to develop systems that test other software. Rather than relying entirely on manual testing, they build automated tests, investigate defects, test APIs, reproduce bugs, and create systems that detect regressions as applications change.

Common technologies include Python, Java, JavaScript, SQL, Selenium, Playwright, Postman, Git, CI/CD tools, APIs, and Docker. This career therefore involves considerably more programming and technical problem-solving than I initially associated with software QA.

I found QA Automation more interesting than some of the other specialized careers because I like debugging and technical problem-solving. However, if given the choice between building a feature and designing systems to test someone else's feature, I would rather build the feature. Testing is something I want to understand and incorporate into my development work, but I do not think I want testing to be the primary purpose of my job.

### What I Learned From the Comparison

Comparing these careers helped me identify that the type of work I want matters more to me than a particular job title. I want to spend a significant portion of my time independently building and solving technical problems while still working as part of a development team and learning from more experienced engineers.

I also realized that my preference for remote work is not only about location. I am looking for a work environment where I can spend substantial periods independently coding and solving problems, while still having a team to collaborate with and more experienced engineers to learn from. I am less interested in positions centered around customers, sales, presentations, or external communities.

My goal with this research was not to choose a career for the rest of my life, but to identify a practical next step after graduation. Based on what I learned, I plan to focus primarily on entry-level Software Engineering/Software Development roles. This path aligns with the kind of work I want to do and builds on many of the skills I already have.

Applied AI/ML Engineering is particularly interesting to me as a possible future direction. I like that it combines software development with AI and machine learning, but many of the roles I researched expect previous software-development experience. Starting in software engineering would allow me to develop that professional foundation while continuing to explore AI. I would also remain open to an entry-level AI-focused opportunity if I found one that matched my skills.

The other careers helped clarify my interests further. I enjoyed the model experimentation and parameter tuning involved in ML/NLP Engineering, but I am less interested in the heavier statistics, data preparation, and data-pipeline responsibilities. Responsible AI is another area I would like to continue exploring because I am interested in questions involving AI bias, limitations, usefulness, safety, and appropriate use. However, I am more interested in these broader questions than in making automated AI evaluation my primary job. I am less interested in specializing in accessibility or software testing, and I do not want a customer-facing technical role such as Solutions Engineering or Developer Relations.

This gives me a clearer direction for my portfolio project. I want to build a polished software application that strengthens my preparation for an entry-level software-development role while also giving me an opportunity to learn technologies I have not had much experience with in my coursework. If it fits naturally with the project, I would also like to incorporate an Applied AI component and begin exploring some of the skills that could eventually help me move toward AI-focused application development.

# Portfolio Project Options

After narrowing my immediate career focus to software development, I began considering portfolio projects that would strengthen my software-engineering skills while also allowing me to explore areas that are new to me. I narrowed my ideas to two projects that approach those goals differently.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>Option 1: Sight-Reading Assessment Application</strong></summary>

The first idea is a mobile-friendly web application for music students practicing sight-reading. The application would present a short musical passage, allow the student to record themselves singing it, analyze the recording using an existing machine-learning/audio-analysis tool, and compare the detected pitches with the expected notes in the passage. The student would then receive visual feedback about their pitch accuracy.

This project would combine my previous experience as a vocal performer and music educator with my current work in computer science. From a technical perspective, it would give me experience building a complete web application, working with browser audio, designing communication between a JavaScript frontend and Python backend, integrating an existing ML-based tool, testing the application, and deploying it.

The largest technical risk is the reliability of extracting musical pitches from a real vocal performance. For that reason, the initial version would focus on short, monophonic vocal passages and pitch accuracy. More complicated features such as rhythmic assessment, progress tracking, practice modes, and a native mobile application could be future extensions.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>Option 2: An Interactive Study Companion for Students</strong></summary>

The second idea is an AI-powered exam preparation application based on a study process I already use. A student would provide course notes and previous assessment material, and the application would use those sources to help them prepare for an upcoming exam.

This application would focus more directly on Applied AI. It could use an LLM API, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), embeddings, vector retrieval, and prompt/context engineering to retrieve relevant information from the student's materials and provide it to a language model. The goal would be to generate explanations and new practice questions that remain grounded in the material actually provided by the student rather than functioning as a generic chatbot.

This project would give me more direct experience with the technologies I encountered while researching Applied AI careers. It would also introduce interesting questions involving hallucinations, source grounding, transparency, and the appropriate use of generated educational material.

</details>

### **Project Direction**

Both project ideas would support my professional-development goals in different ways. The Sight-Reading Assessment Application is particularly appealing because it combines my background in music education and vocal performance with software development. It would also introduce me to audio processing and integrating an existing ML capability into an application. I would still like to develop this project in the future.

For this course, however, I have decided to pursue **StudySync - *AI Study Buddy: An Interactive Study Companion for Students***. One of my goals for this semester project is to take advantage of the structured development time, feedback, and accountability provided by the course to learn technologies that are substantially new to me.

StudySync will allow me to explore several technologies I encountered while researching Applied AI careers, including LLM APIs, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), embeddings, vector retrieval, and prompt/context engineering. At the same time, I will continue developing broader software-engineering skills through frontend/backend integration, API development, testing, and deployment.

The idea comes from a study process I already use. I frequently study by combining my course notes with previous quiz questions and using those materials to generate additional practice and explanations. My goal is to investigate how that workflow can be turned into a focused application that grounds its generated study material in sources provided by the student.

The initial scope will focus specifically on exam preparation rather than creating a general-purpose AI study assistant. A student will provide course materials and previous assessment questions, select material to study, and use StudySync to generate new practice questions and receive explanations grounded in those sources.
